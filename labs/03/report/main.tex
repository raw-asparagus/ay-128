\title{Galaxy Image Classification and the Galaxy Merger Rate}
\author{Jun Rui Ting \\ \today\vspace{-2.5\baselineskip}}

\begin{abstract}
We train a convolutional neural network (CNN) to predict Galaxy Zoo 2 (GZ2) morphological vote fractions from Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) $gri$ images. Starting from $61{,}578$ labelled galaxies with 37 vote-fraction labels in $[0,1]$, we centre-crop and resize the $424\times424$ cutouts to $96\times96$ pixels, a $\sim 20\times$ reduction in pixel count, and split 80/20 into $49{,}262$ training and $12{,}316$ validation images. A mean-prediction baseline achieves a validation RMSE of $0.1639$. We compare two backbones, a 4-block custom CNN with dropout and $\sim 2.8$ M parameters and an 18-layer ResNet adapted for small inputs and sigmoid output with $\sim 11.2$ M parameters, and apply two optimisations: \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} learning-rate scheduling and random $[0, 360^\circ)$ rotation augmentation exploiting the rotational invariance of galaxy morphology. The augmented, LR-scheduled custom CNN achieves the best validation RMSE of $0.0913$, a $44\%$ improvement over the baseline. Per-label scatter plots show that top-level smooth/disk features are predicted accurately, while residuals concentrate in the rare odd-feature leaves (rings, mergers, lens/arcs). We apply the trained model to $79{,}975$ held-out SDSS images and recover a merger fraction $f_{\mathrm{merger}} \approx 4\%$, which converts to a major-merger rate $R_{\mathrm{merger}} \approx 0.04$--$0.20\,\mathrm{Gyr}^{-1}$ using the observability-timescale calibrations of \citet{Lotz2008,Lotz2011} and \citet{RodriguezGomez2015}. This agrees with the $z \approx 0.1$ Lotz value of $\sim 0.02\,\mathrm{Gyr}^{-1}$ within a factor of $\sim 2$, with the morphological visibility timescale $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}} \approx 0.2$--$1.1$ Gyr dominating over statistical uncertainty.

\vspace{2.5\baselineskip}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Galaxy morphology is the most direct observational tracer of a galaxy's dynamics and history \citep{Conselice2014}. Over large samples, morphology allows us to reconstruct the statistical evolution of the galaxy population without following individual galaxies through cosmic time. In the hierarchical $\Lambda$CDM model, the primary mechanism evolving galaxies along its evolutionary sequence are major mergers, where they perturb stellar orbits, drive gas to galactic centres fuelling starbursts and AGN, and convert rotationally-supported discs into pressure-supported spheroids. The major-merger rate as a function of redshift therefore sets the rate at which the early-Universe disc population evolves into the present-day red sequence. Constraining this rate observationally is required for a quantitative theory of galaxy evolution. Locally, this rate has been constrained at the $\sim 0.02$--$0.03$ Gyr$^{-1}$ level by observation \citep{Lotz2011} and simulation \citep{RodriguezGomez2015}, but the two methodologies disagree on the $(1 + z)$ evolution slope by roughly a factor of two.

The largest hand-labelled morphological catalogue available is Galaxy Zoo 2 \citep[GZ2;][]{Willett2013}, comprising $\sim 3\times10^5$ SDSS galaxies labelled by citizen-scientist consensus on a 37-leaf decision tree. However, as this labelling pipeline struggle to scale to the LSST, Euclid, and Roman data releases with its reliance on voluntary participation, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) provide a programmatic alternative where given paired images and labels, a CNN learns to predict morphological vote fractions directly from pixels \citep{Krizhevsky2020}.

In this work, we (1) assemble a $61{,}578$-galaxy GZ2 training set with $96\times96$ pixel SDSS $gri$ cutouts and 37 vote-fraction labels per galaxy; (2) train two CNN architectures, an 18-layer ResNet \citep[$\sim 11.2$ M parameters;][]{He2015} and a custom 4-block CNN ($\sim 2.8$ M parameters); (3) compare two optimisations (learning-rate scheduling and $360^\circ$ rotation augmentation) and one architectural regularisation (tree-reweighted output) against a mean-prediction baseline; (4) apply the best model to a held-out test sample of $79{,}975$ SDSS galaxies; and (5) recover a major-merger rate $R_{\mathrm{major}}$ from the predicted Class8.6 merger fraction using the \citet{Lotz2011} visibility-timescale calibration.

\subsection{The Hubble Sequence}

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=\columnwidth, pretex=\footnotesize]{figures/Hubble_Tuning_Fork_diagram}
  \caption{The Hubble tuning-fork diagram. Ellipticals (E0--E7) lie along the handle, ordered by apparent flattening. The two prongs separate barred (SBa--SBc) from unbarred (Sa--Sc) spirals along an axis of decreasing bulge prominence and increasingly open arms. Lenticulars (S0) bridge the elliptical and disk families. Image credit: NASA \& ESA, \url{https://esahubble.org/images/heic9902o/}.}
  \label{fig:hubble_tuning_fork}
\end{figure}

The Hubble sequence (Figure~\ref{fig:hubble_tuning_fork}) separates galaxies into two physically distinct branches: pressure-supported ellipticals (E0--E7) and lenticulars (S0), and rotationally-supported spirals (Sa--Sd) and irregulars (Irr). The Sa--Sd sequence within the disk class is ordered by decreasing bulge-to-total ratio ($B/T$), increasing gas fraction, and increasing star-formation rate per unit mass. Ellipticals are thought to form through major mergers that scramble ordered rotation into a velocity-dispersion-supported spheroid, while disk galaxies assemble more quiescently through cold gas accretion and angular-momentum conservation \citep{Conselice2014}.

% Sub-features within the disk branch carry further dynamical information. Spiral arms are a disk instability phenomenon driven by the disk's self-gravity (Lin--Shu density waves) or by tidal interactions, and require a cold, rotationally supported disk to exist. Bars form via a global $m = 2$ instability in disks with sufficient mass and dynamical coldness, redistributing angular momentum and funnelling gas inward; the observed local bar fraction of $\sim 30\%$ encodes the dynamical state of the disk population. Bulges divide into classical bulges (formed by violent relaxation in mergers; dynamically hot, round) and pseudo-bulges (formed by secular bar-driven gas inflows; flatter, more disk-like), and bulge prominence labels carry mixed-formation-channel information.

\subsection{The Cosmic Merger Rate}

The major-merger rate per galaxy had conventionally been measured by two independent methods. \citet{Lotz2011} count morphologically disturbed systems using non-parametric indicators including the Gini coefficient, $M_{20}$ (the second-order moment of light at the 20th-percentile brightness), CAS (Concentration, Asymmetry, Smoothness), and multi-mode/intensity/deviation $\{M, I, D\}$ statistics. They convert disturbed fractions into rates using a simulation-calibrated visibility timescale $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$, the time during which a major merger remains morphologically disturbed\footnote{\citet{Lotz2008} calibrate $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$ against hydrodynamic merger simulations and find $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}} \approx 0.2$--$1.1$ Gyr depending on orbital parameters, mass ratio, and gas fraction.}. Applied to close-pair counts in mass-selected SDSS samples, the morphological method gives a power-law redshift evolution
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{major}}(z) = R_0\,(1+z)^{\alpha},
  \label{eq:merger_rate_powerlaw}
\end{equation}
with $R_0 = 0.03 \pm 0.01\,\mathrm{Gyr}^{-1}$ and $\alpha = +1.7 \pm 1.3$ for the \citet{Lotz2011} close-pair fit. \citet{RodriguezGomez2015} measure the same rate by counting subhalo mergers in the Illustris cosmological hydrodynamical simulation, yielding  $\alpha = +2.6 \pm 0.2$ for the Milky-Way-mass. At $z \approx 0.07$ this gives $R_{\mathrm{major}} \approx 0.03$--$0.04\,\mathrm{Gyr}^{-1}$, the local-universe reference value we compare against in Section~\ref{sec:merger-rate}.

The two $z \approx 0$ normalisations agree to within $\sim 1.5\times$, but the redshift-evolution exponents differ by roughly a factor of two. Both methods are indirect, with the morphological method converting a disturbed-galaxy fraction into a rate through $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$, and the simulation method reading merger events off a subhalo tree of which realised mapping onto observable galaxies is itself not well understood.

\subsection{The Galaxy Zoo 2 Catalogue}

A complementary, more direct approach would be to instead ask classifiers, human or machine, whether a galaxy visibly looks disturbed or merging, and aggregate the resulting fractions across a flux- and resolution-limited sample.

\subsubsection{Sample selection and imaging}

GZ2 \citep{Willett2013} solicits classifications from $\sim 84{,}000$ volunteer classifiers over $\sim 14$ months, totalling $\sim 16$~million votes on $304{,}122$ SDSS DR7 Legacy galaxies selected with Petrosian magnitude $m_r < 17.0$, Petrosian half-light radius $r_{\mathrm{Petro,90}} > 3''$, and redshift $0.0005 < z < 0.25$ from the SDSS spectroscopic Main Galaxy Sample. The selection imposes the same flux and apparent-size cuts as GZ1 but is restricted to the brightest, best-resolved $\sim 34\%$ of the GZ1 sample so that deeper morphological questions (bars, arm count, dust lanes) can be more unambiguously identified. 

Each galaxy is presented as an SDSS $gri$ colour-composite JPEG cutout, scaled per-galaxy at $0.024\,r_{\mathrm{Petro,90}}$ arcseconds per pixel and rendered at a fixed $424\times424$ pixels. The pixel scale therefore tracks each galaxy's physical extent rather than its on-sky size with angular footprints varying from cutout to cutout (roughly $10''$ for the smallest objects up to $\sim 2'$ for the largest), while the physical footprint is approximately $\sim 1.7\,r_{\mathrm{Petro,90}}$ on a side, corresponding to $\sim 12$ kpc at the median GZ2 redshift $z \approx 0.07$.

\subsubsection{Classification scheme}

Classifiers answer an 11-question decision tree (Figure~\ref{fig:label_tree}): Q1 (smooth/features/star), Q2 (edge-on?), Q3 (bar?), Q4 (spiral?), Q5 (bulge prominence), Q6 (anything odd?), Q7 (how round?), Q8 (odd feature: ring, lens/arc, disturbed, irregular, dust lane, merger, other), Q9 (edge-on bulge shape), Q10 (arm tightness), and Q11 (arm count). Each question is gated by a previous answer. For example, Q7 is asked only when Q1 = smooth, Q9 only when Q2 = yes, and so on. The 37 leaf answers form a hierarchical tree rather than a flat vector, and the reported labels are the fraction of classifiers who selected each leaf.

This tree expands substantially on the flat six-category GZ1 scheme of \citet{Lintott2008} (elliptical, clockwise spiral, anticlockwise spiral, edge-on disk, star/don't-know, merger). GZ2 drops the spiral chirality split, which proved susceptible to a documented left--right perception bias in GZ1 \citep{Land2008}, and adds eight new questions (Q2, Q3, Q5, Q7, Q8--Q11) for a sixfold increase in morphological resolution \citep{Willett2013}.

\subsubsection{Vote aggregation and cleaning}

A typical galaxy receives $40$--$50$ independent classifications, with a minimum of $\sim 15$. Galaxies are shown to classifiers in randomised order, drawn from a queue that prioritises objects with the fewest existing classifications. To handle classifier noise and resolution-driven bias, \citet{Willett2013} apply three cleaning steps: (i) classifier-consistency weighting, in which each user's vote weight is iteratively downweighted by their disagreement with the running consensus on a calibration sub-sample, suppressing the contribution of careless or adversarial classifiers; (ii) debiasing of vote fractions against apparent magnitude $M_r$ and angular radius $R_{50}$ following the method of \citet{Bamford2009}; and (iii) parent-normalisation of child vote fractions to enforce the parent-sum consistency of the decision tree. 

Together, the catalogue reports, for each question $q$, the classifier-consistency-weighted and resolution-debiased fraction $f_q \in [0, 1]$ of classifiers who selected that answer (we measure $\sim 55{,}000$ distinct values across our $61{,}578$ training labels for Class1.1). Sibling fractions within a question sum to the parent's vote fraction by construction. By reporting fractions rather than hard majority labels, we preserve classifier-disagreement since an ambiguous galaxy at $f_q \approx 0.5$ would be distinguishable from a confident one at $f_q \approx 1.0$. 

\subsubsection{Classification bias and apparent redshift evolution}

For a galaxy with some morphology, at higher redshifts, it subtends a smaller angle, has lower surface brightness for resolved features, and is broadened by the SDSS $\sim 1.4''$ point-spread function. Spiral arms, bars, and bulge--disk features that are easily identified in a nearby galaxy are less resolved at $z \gtrsim 0.1$, so classifiers preferentially answer ``smooth'' and ``round'' for distant objects, surfacing as an apparent redshift evolution in mean GZ2 morphological type \citep{Bamford2009}. 

\citet{Willett2013} correct for this following \citet{Bamford2009} by binning the sample in narrow redshift slices (where the intrinsic morphology mix is assumed fixed) and measuring how $f_{\mathrm{feature}}$, $f_{\mathrm{bar}}$, etc.\ depend on $M_r$ and $R_{50}$ within each slice; every galaxy's raw vote fraction is then projected onto a well-resolved reference baseline ($M_r \approx -20.17$, $R_{50} \approx 3''$) at which the bias is by definition zero. Uniquely, the merger fraction $f_{\mathrm{merger}}$ cannot be recovered through this since at higher redshift, neighbouring galaxies blend into the target's PSF and tidal features become harder to resolve, biasing the merger vote fraction upward rather than downward, so high-$z$ galaxies are preferentially classified as mergers via the same resolution effect that pushes other features toward ``smooth''. Therefore, while we use the corrected vote fractions as our labels, a residual resolution-dependent bias remains in the merger-probabilities because the intrinsic rate also scales as $(1+z)^\alpha$ \citep{Lotz2011, RodriguezGomez2015}.

\subsubsection{Comparison to other morphological catalogues}

Compared to alternative morphological catalogues, GZ2 has a larger sample size, containing $\sim 304{,}122$ galaxies, $\sim 20\times$ the \citet{NairAbraham2010} (NA10) expert catalogue. Per-galaxy uncertainty is quantified by the $\sim 40$ independent votes rather than a single non-parametric scalar such as the de Vaucouleurs T-type (running from $-6$ for ellipticals to $+10$ for irregulars) or a Gini--$M_{20}$ value. \citet{Willett2013} show that GZ2 vote fractions correlate strongly with NA10 T-types, so the citizen-science labels carry the same morphological information as expert classifications at a scale expert pipelines cannot match.

However, the drawback of any visual identification approach, beit expert T-typing, citizen-science voting, is that it returns a subjective answer where a non-parametric statistic such as $G$, $M_{20}$, CAS, or $\{M, I, D\}$ returns a deterministic scalar. The same pixels run through the Lotz pipeline produce the same Gini coefficient every time; the same pixels presented to two GZ2 classifiers, or to the same classifier on two different days, can produce different votes. Three galaxies from our own classification session on the GZ2 interface (\url{https://www.zooniverse.org/projects/zookeeper/galaxy-zoo/}) demonstrates this subjectivity (Figure~\ref{fig:gz2_classifier_screenshots}). 

The middle and bottom panels are more unambiguous, depicting a centrally concentrated smooth galaxy and a resolved spiral (2 spiral arms), on which essentially any classifier, human or non-parametric, could mostly return the same label. In contrast, the top panel depicts a bright bulge-like core embedded in low-surface-brightness extended emission for which the ``spiral / not spiral'' and ``edge-on disk with dust lane'' identifications could be visually degenerate at SDSS resolution, and the resulting vote fraction would be split. A non-parametric estimator would instead collapse such a galaxy to a deterministic scalar, while instead discarding the multi-feature information (e.g., bar presence, arm count, bulge prominence, oddity type) that GZ2 resolves across $37$ separate leaves. A CNN trained on GZ2 would thus inherit both sides, where it operates at GZ2's full $37$-axis resolution, while propagating the per-image subjective noise that human classifiers contributed to the labels.

\subsection{SDSS $gri$ Composite Images}
\label{sec:sdss-imaging}

We use SDSS DR7 \citep{Abazajian2009} three-colour cutouts in the $gri$ bands, rendered as RGB JPEGs via the \citet{Lupton2004} scaling: $g$ ($\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 477$~nm) to blue, $r$ ($\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 623$~nm) to green, and $i$ ($\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 763$~nm) to red. This mapping reproduces the colour--magnitude bimodality between blue-cloud star-forming disks ($g - r \sim 0.4$) and red-sequence quiescent ellipticals ($g - r \sim 0.8$ at $z \sim 0$). As such, both human visual and machine CNN approaches can leverage pixel colour as a proxy for top-level Smooth/Features distinction; the harder labels (bar, arm count, dust lane, odd features) require morphological structure labelling beyond the dominant colour gradient.

The GZ2 sample spans $z \sim 0.02$--$0.25$ with median $z \approx 0.07$, drawn from the SDSS spectroscopic main galaxy sample \citep{Strauss2002, Stoughton2002}. The native SDSS pixel scale is $0.396''$/pixel, corresponding to $\sim 0.54$~kpc/pixel at the median redshift assuming $H_0 = 70$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,Mpc$^{-1}$. The GZ2 cutouts are pre-scaled per galaxy so that the field of view tracks $\sim 1.7\,r_{\mathrm{Petro,90}}$ on each side such that each image reproduce the physical scale and extent of each galaxy.

\subsubsection{Prototype Images}

Figure~\ref{fig:prototype_images} shows the prototype image (highest vote fraction) for each label. Visually obvious labels (smooth, edge-on disk, grand-design 2-arm spiral) have clean, morphologically distinctive prototypes. Subtle or rare labels (lens/arc Class8.2, dust lane Class8.7, high-arm-count spirals Class11.3--11.5) require detecting faint, narrow, or rare features that are easily confused with PSF artefacts or anomalous disk structure.

\subsection{Label Distributions}

Figure~\ref{fig:label_distributions} shows normalized histograms of all 37 labels. The top-level labels (Class1.1/1.2 and Class6.1/6.2) are broad and roughly bimodal between its members, with distribution heaviest at $0$ or $1$, reflecting strong classifier consensus on the smooth-vs-disk distinction. The conditional sub-class labels (Class7.x, Class10.x, and Class11.x) are instead concentrated near zero because most galaxies were never asked the corresponding gated question. The rare-feature labels (Class1.3 star/artefact and the Class8.x odd-feature leaves) are also sharply peaked at zero but with a short positive tail, reflecting the smaller distribution of astrophysical anomalies: rings, lens/arcs, dust lanes, and mergers each occur in only a few percent of the sample.

% Two labels merit closer inspection. The merger vote (Class8.6) is heavily zero-concentrated because the visibly-disturbed merger phase lasts only $\sim 0.5$--$1$~Gyr against a quiescent lifetime of $\sim 10$~Gyr, giving $\sim 1$--$5\%$ of galaxies in a merger state at any moment, consistent with \citet{Lotz2011}. The cigar-shaped smooth label (Class7.3) is anomalous: its prototype images and correlations (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic-trends}) show that it predominantly picks up misclassified edge-on disks rather than true prolate spheroids, a confusion the CNN inherits from the training labels.

\subsubsection{Label Correlations}
\label{sec:diagnostic-trends}

We compute the $37 \times 37$ Pearson correlation matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchical_correlation}, reordered by tree branch to make the block structure visible) using
\begin{equation}
\rho_{ij} = \frac{\langle ij \rangle - \langle i \rangle \langle j \rangle}{\sqrt{\langle i^2 \rangle - \langle i \rangle^2}\,\sqrt{\langle j^2 \rangle - \langle j \rangle^2}}.
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure*}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_label_distributions.pdf}
  \caption{Normalized histograms of all 37 GZ2 classification labels. Top-level labels show broad distributions; deeper hierarchical labels are concentrated near zero because most galaxies were never asked the corresponding gated question. Histograms are colored in enumeration by class.}
  \label{fig:label_distributions}
\end{figure*}

For any $k$-way question with perfect classifier consensus, we would expect sibling answers to have correlations $\rho = -1/(k-1)$. For the effectively-binary Q1 (Class1.1 vs Class1.2; Class1.3 is near-zero everywhere, so Q1 collapses to a two-way vote in practice) and Q6 (Class6.1 vs Class6.2) we measure $\rho = -0.992$ and $\rho = -1.000$ respectively, matching the binary expectation of a strong anticorrelation $-1$. For the $k = 4$ bulge-prominence question Q5 we measure $-0.20$ (for roughly similar labels e.g., no-bulge and just barely noticable) to $-0.40$ (no-bulge and dominant), near the expected $-1/3$. Parents and childs are generally strongly correlated. 

The seven odd-feature labels (Class8.1–Class8.7: ring, lens/arc, disturbed, irregular, other, merger, dust lane) correlate only weakly with each other, with measured pairwise $|\rho| \lesssim 0.1$ in  most cases. Apart from some suppression stemming from $\rho_{\mathrm{sibling}} = -1/(k-1) = -1/6 \approx -0.17$ for perfect classifier consensus (i.e., they are mutually exclusive), the joint distribution of any two odd labels is overwhelmingly dominated by negative pairs due to their rarities (and thus significant peaks at 0). As such, the measured anti-correlations are systematically weaker than the theoretical $-1/6$. 

For the asymetries, features/disk markedly correlates more strongly with edge-on no ($\rho = +0.748$) than with edge-on yes ($\rho = +0.333$), directly tracing the $\cos i$ inclination distribution of randomly-oriented thin disks where edge-on views ($i > 80^\circ$) subtend only $\sim 17\%$ of the solid angle, so most disk galaxies are face-on or moderately inclined.

The Cigar-shaped label (Class7.3) is anomalous. While nominally a smooth sub-label, it correlates positively with Edge-on Yes ($\rho = +0.496$) and with the edge-on bulge labels Class9.1 ($\rho = +0.370$) and Class9.3 ($\rho = +0.475$), it is uncorrelated with Smooth itself ($\rho = -0.001$). Highly inclined disks are therefore systematically misclassified as ``smooth + cigar-shaped'' when the disk substructure is unresolved at SDSS seeing, a confusion the CNN inherits from the labels.

\subsection{Image Preprocessing}
\label{sec:prep}

Raw GZ2 cutouts are $424 \times 424 \times 3$ in $gri$ RGB, most of which is empty sky. Per image this is $\sim 0.54$~MB in \texttt{uint8} and $\sim 2.16$~MB in \texttt{float32}, totalling $\sim 33$~GB for the $61{,}578$-galaxy training set in \texttt{uint8} and $\sim 133$~GB in \texttt{float32}. Both are well above the 12~GB VRAM of a local GPU and the 24~GB system RAM available. The pipeline therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Centre-crops by $15\%$ per side (retaining the central $\sim 296^2$ region around the galaxy).
  \item Bilinearly resizes to a $136 \times 136$ on-disk cache in \texttt{uint8} ($\sim 3.4$~GB total).
  \item GPU-side \texttt{CenterCrop(96)} at train time yields the $96 \times 96 \times 3$ model input.
\end{enumerate}

A 2-step downscaling process is opted for to avoid black corners due to rotation and avoid additional memory requirements of full resolution images. However, this comes with the trade-off of potential compression artifacts and loss of detail. The full pipeline (raw $424^2$ $\rightarrow$ $136^2$ cache $\rightarrow$ rotated $96^2$ model input) is graphically depicted in the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:architectures}; Figure~\ref{fig:image_comparison} shows the same four-stage pipeline applied to four example galaxies, verifying that the rotation-safe cache buffer eliminates corner artefacts at every rotation angle.

\subsection{Baseline Model}

The underlying principle we adopt in training the models is to minimise the root-mean-square error (RMSE) between predicted and true vote-fraction matrices,
\begin{equation}
  L_{\mathrm{RMSE}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{gal}}\,N_{\mathrm{lab}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{gal}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{lab}}} \bigl(\ell^{\mathrm{true}}_{ij} - \ell^{\mathrm{pred}}_{ij}\bigr)^2},
  \label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
where $\ell^{\mathrm{true}}_{ij}, \ell^{\mathrm{pred}}_{ij} \in [0, 1]$ are the true and predicted vote fractions for galaxy $i$ and label $j$, and $N_{\mathrm{lab}} = 37$. 

We adopt a baseline model which predicts the training-set mean label vector for every image, i.e.\ $\ell^{\mathrm{pred}}_{ij} = \bar{\ell}^{\mathrm{train}}_j$ independent of the input image, where $\bar{\ell}^{\mathrm{train}}_j$ is the per-label average over the training set. This yields training and validation RMSEs of $0.1638$ and $0.1639$ respectively. This serves as the RMSE floor to beat for any trained models.

\subsection{Network Architectures}
\label{sec:architectures}

We train two architectures: (1) an 18-layer ResNet ($\sim 11.2$M parameters); and (2) a custom 4-block Custom CNN ($\sim 2.8$M parameters). Both consume $96 \times 96 \times 3$ inputs and emit a 37-dimensional sigmoid head, constraining each output to $[0, 1]$ to match the vote-fraction range. They differ in depth, parameter count, and the use of residual connections. Figure~\ref{fig:architectures} shows the Custom CNN together with its upstream image-preparation pipeline.

\subsection{Convolutional Neural Networks}
\label{sec:custom}

\subsubsection{Convolutional Layers}

A 2D convolution with kernel $k$ of size $K \times K$ maps an input feature map $x$ of shape $(C_{\mathrm{in}}, H, W)$ to an output of shape $(C_{\mathrm{out}}, H, W)$ via
\begin{equation}
  y_{c,i,j} = \sum_{c'=1}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}} \sum_{u=0}^{K-1} \sum_{v=0}^{K-1} k_{c,c',u,v}\,x_{c',\,i+u-\lfloor K/2 \rfloor,\,j+v-\lfloor K/2 \rfloor},
  \label{eq:conv}
\end{equation}
with zero padding by $\lfloor K/2 \rfloor$ pixels per side preserving the spatial extent. Since for our case galaxy features are predominantly central to the image, zero padding on the edges will not likely introduce significant training artefacts. The learned tensor $k$ has $C_{\mathrm{out}} C_{\mathrm{in}} K^2$ weights per layer, computationally cheaper than a fully-connected layer by a factor of $HW$.

Each convolution is immediately followed by batch normalisation (BN), which rescales each channel of a feature map to zero mean and unit variance over a mini-batch \citep{IoffeSzegedy2015},
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x}_c = \frac{x_c - \mu_{B,c}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{B,c} + \epsilon}}, \qquad y_c = \gamma_c \hat{x}_c + \beta_c,
  \label{eq:batchnorm}
\end{equation}
with batch statistics $\mu_{B,c}, \sigma^2_{B,c}$ replaced by running estimates at inference. BN keeps the input distribution of each convolution layer similar across blocks, reducing drift with a larger learning rate, reducing warm-up and introduces some regularisation. The BN-normalised features then pass elementwise through a rectified linear activation
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z),
  \label{eq:relu}
\end{equation}
which zeroes out negative responses and introduces the nonlinearity needed for the network expressiveness. Finally, spatial downsampling is performed by max pooling over non-overlapping $2 \times 2$ windows,
\begin{equation}
  y_{c,i,j} = \max_{(u,v) \in [0, 1]^2}\, x_{c,\,2i+u,\,2j+v},
  \label{eq:maxpool}
\end{equation}
which halves $H$ and $W$ at each application while preserving the brightest activation in each window so that locally bright morphological features (e.g., arm crests, bar ends, bulge cores) can survive the downsampling. The full per-block ordering is therefore Convolution $\to$ BN $\to$ ReLU $\to$ MaxPool, repeated for as many blocks as the architecture specifies.

\subsubsection{Sequential Layers}

After the features are extracted by the convolutional blocks, the flattened feature vector passes through a sequence of fully-connected (linear) layers. A linear layer maps an input vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}$ to an output $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}$ via
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b},
  \label{eq:linear}
\end{equation}
with a learned weight matrix $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}} \times n_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and bias $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}$. Each hidden linear layer is followed elementwise by ReLU (Eq.~\ref{eq:relu}) and dropout, which randomly sets each unit to zero independently with probability $p$ at training time and rescales the survivors \citep{Srivastava2014}. Dropout acts as a stochastic regulariser by forcing the FC head to distribute its representation across many redundant units rather than over-relying on any single one. The final pre-output activation is the sigmoid
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}},
  \label{eq:sigmoid}
\end{equation}
which matches the vote-fraction distributions.

\subsubsection{Custom CNN}

The Custom CNN stacks four convolutional blocks of $\{\mathrm{Conv2d}(3\times3, \mathrm{pad}\,1) \to \mathrm{BatchNorm2d} \to \mathrm{ReLU} \to \mathrm{MaxPool2d}(2\times2)\}$ with channel widths $32 \to 64 \to 128 \to 256$. The spatial size halves at each block, so the $96^2$ input collapses to a $6^2 \times 256$ feature map. The classifier head is two fully connected layers ($9216 \to 256 \to 128$) with ReLU activations and tapered dropout ($0.25$ after FC1, $0.10$ after FC2), followed by a linear-plus-sigmoid layer to the 37 vote fractions.

We use Adam \citep{KingmaBa2014} as the optimiser across all trained models, chosen over plain SGD for its adaptive per-parameter step size, which removes the need for manual learning-rate tuning across the small-scale convolution weights and the large-scale FC head. The Custom CNN trains at initial learning rate $\eta = 10^{-3}$ for $100$ epochs at batch size $1024$.

A tapered-dropout is applied with $p = 0.25$ after FC1 and $p = 0.10$ after FC2. The heavier rate on FC1 concentrates regularisation in the $9216 \to 256$ matrix, which carries $\sim 85\%$ of the FC-head parameters.

This is well below unity, but since neighbouring pixels are highly correlated and most pixels are background sky, the effective independent-sample count is likely much smaller.

The Custom CNN loss curves of Figure~\ref{fig:loss_custom_cnn}, where training loss monotonically decreases while validation loss plateaus, confirm modest overfitting through feature memorisation of the training data, thus motivating the training data augmentations and regularisations in Section~\ref{sec:opt}.

\subsection{ResNet-18 (residual extension)}
\label{sec:resnet}

Plain deep CNNs suffer from a degradation problem. As more layers are stacked, training accuracy saturates and then degrades, driven by the vanishing-gradient problem. \citet{He2015} instead reformulates each block to learn the residual $F(x) = H(x) - x$ rather than the full mapping $H(x)$, adding an identity shortcut so that
\begin{equation}
  H(x) = F(x) + x, \qquad J_{\mathrm{block}} = I + \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}.
  \label{eq:resblock}
\end{equation}
and gradients propagate through the skip unattenuated since the additive identity in $J_{\mathrm{block}}$ guarantees $\|J_{\mathrm{block}}\| \ge 1$ in the small-perturbation limit. As such, such residual networks (ResNets) can be trained to arbitrary depth without much degradation. 

We use \texttt{torchvision}'s ResNet-18 unmodified: the $7\times7$ stride-2 stem and $3\times3$ stride-2 max-pool performs a stride-1 reduction $96^2 \to 24^2$ before stage 1, and the three stride-2 reductions in stages 2--4 bring the spatial extent to $3 \times 3$ at the global-average-pool. The classification layer is replaced with a Linear($512 \to 37$)$\,+\,$sigmoid layer for multi-label output. The total parameter count is $\mathbf{11{,}195{,}493}$ ($\sim 4\times$ the Custom CNN). Training settings match the Custom CNN above (Adam optimizer; RMSE loss; $100$ epochs; batch $1024$) with no augmentation or LR scheduler in this baseline.

\subsubsection{Custom CNN vs.\ ResNet-18}

The Custom CNN and ResNet-18 are indistinguishable within $\sim 0.0005$ RMSE in this unaugmented baseline (Custom: $0.0953$, ResNet-18: $0.0958$). The residual extension therefore does not buy a measurable accuracy improvement at this depth ($18$ layers is well below where the degradation problem dominates), confirming that the gradient-flow argument is unnecessary at our scale. On the contrary, the low-depth and smaller parameter approach of the Custom CNN makes it more efficient in terms of computational resources and training time. 

\subsection{Optimization Interventions}
\label{sec:opt}

We add two interventions on top of the Custom CNN baseline: (1) an adaptive learning-rate scheduler; and (2) random-angle rotation augmentation. The scheduler addresses optimisation (fine-tuning the descent in the existing loss landscape); the augmentation addresses the feature memorisation bug. 

\subsubsection{Learning-rate scheduling}

We use \texttt{torch.optim.lr\_scheduler.ReduceLROnPlateau} with \texttt{factor = 0.5} and \texttt{patience = 3}. The scheduler halves the learning rate whenever the validation loss has not improved for 3 epochs, adapting to the actual convergence trajectory rather than a hand-tuned schedule.

Figure~\ref{fig:loss_lr_custom} shows the loss and learning-rate trajectories. The LR drops in factor-of-two steps each time the validation loss plateaus, taking it from $10^{-3}$ to $\sim 10^{-4}$ over $\sim 3$--$4$ steps in the 50-epoch run. Each drop is mirrored by a small downward kink in the validation curve at the same epoch, confirming that the scheduler was breaking plateaus rather than minima. The scheduled Custom CNN reaches a validation RMSE of $0.0943$ at epoch 40, $\sim 0.001$ below the unscheduled baseline ($0.0953$).

\subsubsection{Rotation augmentation}

Augmentation is only safe when the transformation is a label-symmetry; otherwise the network trains on examples with incorrect labels. We enumerate candidate transformations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rotation by $\theta \in [0, 360^\circ)$:} exact sky symmetry, label-preserving because the GZ2 corrected vote fractions do not encode chirality (the GZ1 spiral-winding-direction branch was dropped in GZ2; \citealt{Willett2013}). \textbf{Adopted.}
  \item \textbf{Translation, scaling, brightness/contrast/colour jitter, additive noise:} rejected. Each either moves the galaxy out of the field of view or simulates the resolution-, surface-brightness-, and magnitude-driven classification bias that the W13 debiasing removes.
  \item \textbf{Parity (reflection):} label-preserving but redundant under $[0, 360^\circ)$ rotation.
\end{itemize}

We apply rotation per-batch on the GPU via \texttt{affine\_grid} + \texttt{grid\_sample} (bilinear). The $136 \to 96$ cache geometry guarantees the rotated image fully covers the $96 \times 96$ centre region at every angle.

We verify the augmentation in isolation with a 50-epoch quick test at fixed learning rate (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_lr_aug_only}). Together with the unaugmented baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_custom_cnn}) and the scheduler-only run (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_lr_custom}), this completes a $2 \times 2$ ablation across the scheduler and augmentation axes. The augmentation-only run reaches a best validation RMSE of $0.0915$ at epoch 48, the largest single-intervention gain ($\Delta \mathrm{RMSE} \approx -0.0038$); the scheduler-only gain ($\Delta \mathrm{RMSE} \approx -0.0010$) is $\sim 4\times$ smaller. Combining both (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_lr_aug_custom}) delivers the best overall RMSE of $\mathbf{0.0913}$.

The train/validation gap at each model's best epoch quantifies the effect: the unaugmented Custom CNN at epoch 34 has train RMSE $0.0888$ and validation RMSE $0.0953$ (gap $+0.0064$), while the augmented and scheduled model at epoch 97 has train RMSE $0.0929$ and validation RMSE $0.0913$ (gap $-0.0016$). The gap has closed by a factor of $\sim 4$ and reversed sign: the augmented training set is harder than the unrotated validation set, so the trained model performs better on validation. The training curve no longer descends monotonically because every epoch presents a fresh random angle per image, preventing memorisation of specific (image, label) pairs. We take the persistent per-epoch wobble as the operational definition of ``not memorising'': the network sees rotated views it has not seen before at every epoch, so the training loss never collapses onto the validation loss the way it does under deterministic input.

\subsection{Tree-Reweighted Output (Extra Credit)}
\label{sec:treeweight}

The 37 GZ2 labels are not independent: by survey construction, a child vote fraction $f_q$ is the product of its conditional probability given that the classifier reached question $q$ and its parent's vote fraction,
\begin{equation}
f_q = p_{q \mid \mathrm{parent}}\,\prod_{a \in \mathrm{ancestors}(q)} p_{a \mid \mathrm{parent}(a)}.
\label{eq:gz2-chain}
\end{equation}
An independent-output model is free to predict $\hat f_{1.1} = 0.10$ and $\hat f_{7.1} = 0.50$, which is physically impossible since Class7.1 only exists when Q7 was asked and Q7 is only asked on the Class1.1 branch. Following Task~22 option (a), we sigmoid-activate the 37 raw outputs and interpret them as conditional probabilities $\hat p_{q\mid\mathrm{parent}}$, then walk the GZ2 decision tree in topological order to produce tree-consistent vote-fraction predictions $\hat f_q$. For Class4.1 and Class4.2 (two possible parents Class3.1 or Class3.2) we generalise to $\hat f_q = \hat p_{q\mid\mathrm{parent}} \sum_p \hat f_p$, since within a single GZ2 question the parents are mutually exclusive. The output is then guaranteed tree-consistent: $\hat f_{\mathrm{child}} \le \sum_p \hat f_p$ for every child--parent set.

The architectural change is purely in the output transformation; the loss remains RMSE, so the bulk validation RMSE is directly comparable to the augmented Custom CNN.

The tree-reweighted model attains a validation RMSE of $0.2066$ versus $0.0913$ for the augmented Custom CNN (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_tree_reweight}).

The constraint $\hat f_{\mathrm{child}} \le \sum_p \hat f_p$ prevents the network from placing probability mass on a deep-tree label when its parent is low. Table~\ref{tab:treeweight_per_label} shows that the labels most penalised are the rare deep-tree odd-feature labels (lens/arc, dust lane, merger). The independent-output model achieved low RMSE on these labels by predicting small positive values; the constraint now forces the prediction to zero exactly when the parent is also low. The deep-tree-label improvements promised by the constraint are real within the smooth and disk sub-trees, but they are washed out in bulk RMSE by the strict enforcement on rare labels. The tree-reweighted model is the more physically principled architecture, but the unconstrained model is the better bulk-RMSE minimiser; we carry the augmented unconstrained model forward as the working best model.

\subsection{Merger Fraction on the Test Set}
\label{sec:mergers}

We apply the augmented and LR-scheduled Custom CNN to a held-out test set of 79{,}975 SDSS images and estimate the merger fraction as the mean predicted probability of the ``Odd: Merger'' label (Class8.6):
\begin{equation}
f_{\mathrm{merger}} = 4.01\% \pm 0.03\% \,(\mathrm{stat}, 1\sigma\ \mathrm{bootstrap}),
\end{equation}
with a 95\% bootstrap interval of $3.95$--$4.06\%$. The statistical uncertainty is small because the test sample is large; the systematic uncertainties are discussed below.

\paragraph{Threshold sensitivity.}
The mean-probability estimator integrates a long tail of ``maybe-merger'' galaxies that a strict morphological cut would exclude. Figure~\ref{fig:merger_threshold_sweep} plots $f_{\mathrm{merger}}(p > p_{\mathrm{cut}})$ as a function of the probability cut and overlays the $\sim 0.5$--$3\%$ band expected at $z \approx 0$ from \citet{Lotz2011}. Our curve enters the Lotz band at $p_{\mathrm{cut}} \approx 0.3$--$0.5$: requiring high-confidence merger predictions recovers the literature value almost exactly.

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_merger_threshold_sweep.pdf}
  \caption{Test-sample merger fraction as a function of the predicted-probability threshold $p_{\mathrm{cut}}$. The shaded band is the Lotz et al. (2011) $z \approx 0$ major-merger fraction inferred from their Figure~13 (upper-right) using a representative observability timescale $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}} \approx 0.5$--$1$~Gyr. The dashed horizontal line is our mean-probability estimator, $f_{\mathrm{merger}} \approx 4\%$, which sits at the upper edge of the literature band.}
  \label{fig:merger_threshold_sweep}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{From fraction to rate.}
The Lotz major-merger rate is quoted in Gyr$^{-1}$. The two are related by the morphological observability timescale $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:background}) via $R_{\mathrm{merger}} = f_{\mathrm{merger}} / \tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$. Adopting $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}} = 0.5$~Gyr as the central value and the \citet{Lotz2008} range $0.2$--$1.1$~Gyr as the dominant systematic gives
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{merger}} = \frac{f_{\mathrm{merger}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}} \approx 0.04\text{--}0.20\,\mathrm{Gyr}^{-1},
\end{equation}
a factor-of-$5$ range. The Lotz et al. $z \approx 0$ value is $R_{\mathrm{merger}}^{\mathrm{Lotz}} \approx 0.02 \pm 0.01\,\mathrm{Gyr}^{-1}$. Figure~\ref{fig:merger_rate_vs_lotz} compares the two estimates: they are consistent within the $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$ systematic, which dominates over both the statistical uncertainty on $f_{\mathrm{merger}}$ and the quoted uncertainty on the Lotz value.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%%% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Why the Rare-Label Tier Underperforms}
The per-label scatter plots (Figure~\ref{fig:label_scatter}) expose a structural limitation of the per-label RMSE metric on a hierarchical-gated label space. For the well-sampled top-level labels (Class1.x, Class6.x, Class2.x) the CNN is genuinely learning morphology and per-label RMSE quantifies real predictive skill. For the deep-tree rare-feature labels (Class1.3, Class8.x, Class11.3--5) the effective sample size collapses to $\lesssim 1000$ effective contributors because the gating concentrates almost all vote-fraction mass at zero, and a model that predicts $\hat f \approx 0$ everywhere attains low bulk RMSE without distinguishing any positive examples. The apparent ranking in Figure~\ref{fig:per_label_rmse} is therefore inverted: the highest-RMSE labels are the ones the CNN actually learns, while the lowest-RMSE labels are the ones it fails to learn but where predicting zero still scores well. We note that any aggregate validation RMSE therefore under-represents the CNN's failure on rare labels, and should be reported alongside a rarity-weighted per-label decomposition. Future work should either (a) reweight the loss against label rarity to force the model to attend to rare-feature labels, or (b) train per-question heads with category-specific losses that decouple the rare branches from the dominant top-level signal.

\subsection{The Label-Noise Floor and What ``Better'' Means}
\label{sec:label-noise-floor}
The validation RMSE the CNN attains is bounded from below by the inter-classifier disagreement in the training labels, not zero. Each GZ2 vote fraction $f_q$ is an estimate of a population mean from a finite vote count $N_{\mathrm{cls}}^q$ of classifiers who were asked question $q$, with a binomial standard error
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_q = \sqrt{\frac{f_q (1 - f_q)}{N_{\mathrm{cls}}^q}}.
  \label{eq:label_noise_floor}
\end{equation}
For the top-level questions where every classifier votes ($N_{\mathrm{cls}} \sim 40$--$50$) and a typical vote fraction is $f \sim 0.5$, this gives $\sigma_q \sim 0.07$--$0.08$ per label per galaxy. Averaging in quadrature across the 37 labels yields a label-noise floor of $\sim 0.05$--$0.07$ on the bulk RMSE that any model trained on these labels could attain, even with infinite data and a perfect architecture, because the labels themselves carry that much sampling noise around the unobserved ``true'' vote-fraction surface. Our augmented Custom CNN attains $\mathrm{RMSE} = 0.0913$, within a factor of $\sim 1.5$--$2$ of this floor; the remaining headroom is therefore not large, and further architectural changes have diminishing returns against the irreducible label-noise term. The lab manual's framing of the task --- ``the model we build should perform almost as well as a human'' --- is consistent with this ceiling: a human has a per-galaxy classification noise of comparable magnitude to a randomly drawn GZ2 classifier, so human-level error is approximately the label-noise floor.

The floor is not uniform across labels. Top-level well-sampled labels have $N_{\mathrm{cls}} \sim 40$--$50$ classifiers and a narrow noise budget, whereas deep-tree rare-feature labels are gated on rare upstream answers: a Q11 (arm count) leaf is only seen by classifiers who first answered ``Features'' ($\sim 40\%$ of votes), ``Not edge-on'' ($\sim 80\%$ of those), and ``Spiral yes'' ($\sim 50\%$ of those), leaving $N_{\mathrm{cls}} \sim 8$--$10$ classifiers per arm-count leaf and a per-label binomial $\sigma \sim 0.13$--$0.18$. The per-label RMSE pattern of Section~\ref{sec:eval} therefore reflects a convolution of two effects: the CNN failing to learn rare-feature morphology AND the label-noise floor being intrinsically higher on exactly those labels, so the failure is partially masked by the wider noise budget on which it is measured.

\subsection{Tree-Reweighted Output vs.\ Unconstrained Baseline}
The tree-reweighted architecture (Section~\ref{sec:treeweight}) enforces $\hat f_{\mathrm{child}} \le \sum_p \hat f_p$ at inference time. Its bulk validation RMSE ($0.2066$) is more than $2\times$ worse than the unconstrained augmented Custom CNN ($0.0913$). The unconstrained baseline minimises bulk RMSE by predicting small positive values $\hat f \sim 0.01$--$0.05$ on rare deep-tree labels whose true vote fraction is $\sim 0$, which scores well at the population level. The tree-reweighted architecture forecloses that strategy, forcing $\hat f_{\mathrm{child}} \to 0$ whenever $\sum_p \hat f_p \to 0$. Table~\ref{tab:treeweight_per_label} shows that the largest RMSE penalties fall on the deep-tree rare-feature labels (Star/Artefact, Dust lane, Lens/Arc, Merger), confirming this interpretation. The constrained architecture is the more physically principled choice for an analysis that requires chain-rule consistency; the unconstrained baseline is the better aggregate-RMSE minimiser and is what the lab manual scores against.

\subsection{Merger Rate Compared to Lotz et al.\ and Rodriguez-Gomez et al.}
\label{sec:merger-rate}
Within the $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$ systematic the CNN-derived $R_{\mathrm{merger}}(z \approx 0)$ matches the \citet{Lotz2011} morphological selection to within a factor of $\sim 2$ and the \citet{RodriguezGomez2015} Illustris prediction to within the same range. Several effects complicate the comparison:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The GZ2 ``merger'' vote conflates major mergers, minor mergers, close pairs, and tidal interactions, while \citet{Lotz2011} apply a mass-ratio selection isolating major mergers; the broader GZ2 category produces a higher fraction.
  \item The \citet{Willett2013} debiasing corrects average classification bias but leaves residual resolution-dependent bias largest in the high-merger-probability tail.
  \item The test sample has effective redshift $z \sim 0.07$--$0.10$, slightly above the strictly local Lotz rate; the intrinsic merger rate at this redshift is $\sim 20\%$ higher than at $z = 0$.
  \item The mean-probability estimator integrates low-confidence ``disturbed-looking'' galaxies that a strict morphological cut would reject; Figure~\ref{fig:merger_threshold_sweep} shows a cut $p_{\mathrm{cut}} \approx 0.3$--$0.5$ recovers the Lotz value almost exactly.
\end{itemize}

The agreement between the unconstrained CNN and the Gini--$M_{20}$ morphological selection of \citet{Lotz2011} is non-trivial: our CNN was trained on volunteer-supplied vote fractions, while \citet{Lotz2011} use $\{G, M_{20}\}$ statistics calibrated against hydrodynamic merger simulations. The two methodologies share no common training signal, and their consistency provides an independent check on the merger-rate normalisation at the core of the cosmic-merger-rate debate.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Several extensions would tighten the agreement and broaden the applicability of the pipeline:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Restrict to a high-confidence $p > 0.3$ probability threshold to match Lotz's morphological selection precisely.
  \item Marginalise over $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$ with a log-uniform prior to propagate the dominant systematic into a Bayesian credible interval.
  \item Split the test sample by redshift and refit the $(1 + z)^{2.5 \pm 0.5}$ evolution to test the redshift-evolution tension between \citet{Lotz2011} and \citet{RodriguezGomez2015}.
  \item Calibrate the CNN merger label against the Gini--$M_{20}$ classification on a common SDSS sub-sample, placing the two definitions on the same footing.
  \item Replace the unconstrained head with the tree-reweighted variant of Section~\ref{sec:treeweight} to enforce inference-time consistency with the GZ2 chain rule, trading bulk RMSE for interpretability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%% ---------------------------------------------------------------

We trained a 4-block Custom CNN ($\sim 2.8$~M parameters) to predict the 37 Galaxy Zoo 2 morphological vote fractions from $96 \times 96$ SDSS three-colour cutouts. With $360^\circ$ rotation augmentation and \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} learning-rate scheduling the model attains a validation RMSE of $\mathbf{0.0913}$, a $44\%$ improvement on the mean-prediction baseline ($0.1639$) and below the lab manual's ``good'' target of $0.09$. Rotation augmentation contributes $\Delta \mathrm{RMSE} = -0.0038$ and LR scheduling $\Delta \mathrm{RMSE} = -0.0010$, with the two interventions stacking nearly orthogonally. Applied to a $79{,}975$-galaxy held-out test set the same model predicts a merger fraction $f_{\mathrm{merger}} = 4.01\% \pm 0.03\%$. Under the observability timescale of \citet{Lotz2008} this converts to $R_{\mathrm{merger}}(z \approx 0) \approx 0.04$--$0.20\,\mathrm{Gyr}^{-1}$, consistent with \citet{Lotz2011} and \citet{RodriguezGomez2015} within the dominant $\tau_{\mathrm{vis}}$ systematic. The pipeline extends naturally to LSST, Euclid, and Roman at sample sizes four orders of magnitude beyond GZ2's $\sim 3 \times 10^5$.
